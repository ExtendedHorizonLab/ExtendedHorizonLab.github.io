Bridging AI, HCI, the Learning Sciences, and Educational Practice

AI-supported learning sits at the intersection of AI capabilities, interaction design, learning processes, and educational practice. This intersection is reflected in work on real-time classroom orchestration, AI-assisted learning analytics for collaborative programming, the fit between emerging interactive technologies and educators' pedagogical practices, and student-centered evaluations of generative AI feedback. Yet interdisciplinary collaboration remains challenging: collaborators may differ in how they frame problems, select methods, evaluate evidence, and involve educational stakeholders.

Building on our 2026 workshop at the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIED), this CHI edition shifts attention from primarily discussing AI-supported learning systems to the collaboration processes that make such work possible. CHI 2027's theme of bridges explicitly connects disciplines and research with practice. HAIL provides a focused setting for examining how these bridges are built, where collaboration breaks down, and what practices and infrastructures can support sustained interdisciplinary work. Through thematic presentations, structured cross-community discussions, and small-group activities, participants will examine recurring collaboration challenges and promising practices, and the workshop will produce a participant-informed synthesis of challenges, practices, and open questions for future interdisciplinary work.

Guiding Questions

  • Shared research problems. How can collaborators develop shared research problems across AI, HCI, and the learning sciences? How can technical opportunities, human needs, and learning goals be negotiated into questions that different communities can jointly pursue?
  • Methods and evidence. How can collaborators integrate methods and forms of evidence across communities? Where do computational, interaction, and educational approaches complement one another, and where do their assumptions or standards of evidence differ?
  • Educational practitioners and learners. How can educational practitioners and learners meaningfully shape interdisciplinary research? How can their situated knowledge inform problem formulation, system design, and evaluation?
  • Sustained collaboration. What enables interdisciplinary collaborations to persist beyond individual projects? What practices, shared resources, infrastructures, and community structures support sustained collaboration?

Previous Edition

Our first workshop, Designing and Evaluating Next-Generation Learning Interfaces: Linking AI, HCI, and the Learning Sciences, took place at AIED 2026 and attracted 62 participants, with 19 accepted contributions. Its panel, presentations, demonstrations, and community discussion showed substantial interest in connecting perspectives across AI, HCI, and the learning sciences. Participants also surfaced differences in terminology, problem framing, and standards of evidence, as well as disciplinary silos and the need for stronger participation from educational practice. These observations directly shaped the focus of this CHI edition.
